## The allocation is only half the strategy

Every Canadian investor eventually discovers the same uncomfortable truth: two
portfolios holding *identical ETFs in identical proportions* can still end up
with meaningfully different after-tax wealth. The difference is not *what* they
hold. It is *where* they hold it — and how much of their return leaks to
withholding taxes, dividend taxation, and capital-gains inclusion before it
ever reaches their account statement.

This article walks through a complete PySharpe pipeline: a four-ETF Canadian
portfolio — **VFV.TO** (S&P 500), **VCN.TO** (TSX Composite), **VDY.TO** (TSX
high dividend), and **QQC.TO** (NASDAQ-100) — allocated across a **TFSA**, an
**RRSP**, and a **Non-Registered** account. We follow the data from raw
yfinance downloads to a validated, tax-adjusted buy plan, and we finish with
the part most quant projects skip: the ledger that proves the strategy is not
overfit.

## The scenario

```bash
# data/portfolio/cad_showcase.csv
VFV.TO
VCN.TO
VDY.TO
QQC.TO
```

Three accounts, three different tax regimes:

| Account | US FWT on dividends | Annual income taxation |
| ------- | ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| TFSA | Lost (no treaty protection) | None |
| RRSP | Treaty-protected for US-domiciled holdings | Deferred until withdrawal |
| Non-Registered | Recoverable via Foreign Tax Credit | Taxed at marginal rates |

The naive approach treats these as decoration. PySharpe treats them as a
second optimization problem running *orthogonally* to asset allocation.

## Stage 1 — Data with a memory: DuckDB write-through caching

Every price series starts at yfinance, but it is never used raw. The pipeline
writes each download through a DuckDB cache keyed by ticker, period, and date
range, with a 24-hour freshness window and file-`mtime`-based invalidation on
collation. Repeat runs are SQL-fast instead of network-slow, and a stale price
is structurally impossible.

Then the harder part: currency. VFV.TO and QQC.TO are CAD-denominated, but
their underlying exposure is USD. The FX layer converts using CAD/USD rates
and applies a strict guardrail: **rows without FX rate coverage are excluded,
never backfilled**. A `.bfill()` here would silently fabricate prices the
investor could not actually have traded at — a classic lookahead leak that
inflates Sharpe ratios in backtests.

## Stage 2 — Estimation and the efficient frontier

With clean, collated prices in hand, PySharpe estimates the inputs to
optimization:

- **Expected returns** use the default Bayes-Stein shrinkage estimator
  (Jorion 1986), which pulls each asset's noisy sample mean toward the
  cross-sectional grand mean. The shrinkage intensity is data-driven:
  similar-looking assets are shrunk aggressively, genuinely different ones
  are shrunk less.
- **Covariance** uses analytical Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage, eigen-clipped to
  guarantee strict positive-definiteness — solvers never see a singular
  matrix, and a near-singular sample covariance can never silently collapse
  the frontier into an equal-weight point.

The max-Sharpe optimizer then sweeps the feasible region subject to MER caps
and geographic bounds. For this scenario the unconstrained solution tends to
concentrate in US equities — which is exactly when the second dimension has
to intervene.

## Stage 3 — The 2-D Asset Location Engine

This is where PySharpe diverges from a standard Markowitz workflow. The
`AssetLocationEngine` in `src/pysharpe/optimization/tax_location.py` solves
asset allocation *and* account placement simultaneously, producing a
tax-adjusted expected return for every `(asset, account)` pair:

```text
R_i,A = R_i − MER_i − Drag_i,A
```

The drag term combines two structurally different taxes.

### Foreign withholding tax: the 15% leak

US-source dividends are subject to a 15% US withholding tax. Whether that
leak is recoverable depends on the account, and the engine encodes the three
Canadian structural rules precisely:

1. **RRSP / LIRA / RRIF** — the US-Canada treaty exempts *directly held*
   US-domiciled assets (e.g., VOO). Drag: **0**.
2. **CAD-wrapped US ETFs (VFV.TO, QQC.TO)** — the fund itself pays the 15%
   FWT before distributing. That fund-level tax is **unrecoverable in every
   account type**, including the RRSP. Drag: `dividend_yield × 0.15`.
3. **TFSA / FHSA** — no treaty protection. Both direct and CAD-wrapped US
   exposure incur `dividend_yield × 0.15`, unrecoverable.
4. **Non-Registered** — FWT is recoverable through the Foreign Tax Credit,
   but the dividend itself is then taxed at marginal rates.

The crucial nuance: **switching VFV.TO into the RRSP does nothing**. The
engine's distinguishing signal is that VOO in the RRSP pays zero FWT, while
VFV.TO pays 19.5 basis points per year regardless of account (at a 1.3%
yield). The location matrix makes this visible per asset, per account:

### Income tax: the Non-Registered penalty

Outside registered accounts, the engine layers the investor's `TaxProfile`
(45% marginal rate in this scenario) on top of the income mix of each asset:

- Interest and foreign income → taxed at the full marginal rate.
- Eligible Canadian dividends (VDY.TO's 4.3% yield) → taxed through the 38%
  gross-up and dividend tax credit, for an effective rate near 27.6%.
- Capital gains → taxed at `marginal_rate × inclusion_rate`, i.e. 22.5% at
  the 50% inclusion rate.

The result is an adjusted expected return per `(asset, account)` pair. The
engine then scores tax efficiency in `[0, 1]` and blends it into the
allocation opportunity scores — so the optimizer naturally routes Canadian
dividend payers into the TFSA, leaves the RRSP for the assets that benefit
most from deferral, and accepts the tax drag in Non-Registered only where
the strategy requires it.

## Stage 4 — From weights to a buy plan

The optimized, tax-adjusted weights are not a report; they are an order list.
The rebalancer compares current holdings against targets, scores
opportunities with the 60/40 Value Averaging blend (60% path drift, 40%
valuation/mean-reversion), and emits a per-account plan with whole-share
rounding, leftover-cash accounting, and commission/slippage estimates —
ready to execute in an iPad-sized table rather than a spreadsheet maze.

## The validation ledger: proving the edge survives out-of-sample

A frontier is a statement about the past. The reason to trust it is the
validation stack that surrounds it — and PySharpe makes that stack
*auditable*, not just present.

Every optimization trial is written to a **DuckDB-backed trial ledger**
(`src/pysharpe/validation/ledger.py`) with its full configuration stored as
JSON, its in-sample and out-of-sample Sharpe ratios, an economic-justification
verdict, and an execution status. Failed and rejected trials are recorded
alongside successful ones — nothing is filtered from the record.

Before a trial is even run, a **pre-flight economic check** rejects
parameterizations that contradict their declared strategy philosophy: a
"trend-following" configuration with a negative momentum sign is flagged
before it can burn compute, let alone look impressive in hindsight.

Out-of-sample honesty comes from a walk-forward harness with purged,
embargoed folds. Each rolling window trains an optimizer on strictly past
data and simulates the resulting weights on the subsequent out-of-sample
window — never using a future price or cost observation. The purge and
embargo gaps are not arbitrary constants; they are sized from the asset's
own autocorrelation decay (`autocorrelation_decay_lag`, threshold |ρ| < 0.05,
taking the maximum lag across all assets). Observations closer than the
decay lag are serially dependent on the test fold, so they are excluded
from training, and adjacent test folds are guaranteed to be separated by at
least the embargo size.

Two deflation layers then attack the two ways strategies fake an edge:

- The **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** scales the observed Sharpe by √θ (Lo 2002),
  penalizing autocorrelated returns that inflate apparent performance.
- The **Probability of Backtest Overfitting** (Bailey & López de Prado 2014)
  uses the logit transform of the Spearman rank correlation between
  in-sample and out-of-sample Sharpe ratios: if in-sample rank predicts
  out-of-sample rank, PBO → 0 and the strategy has genuine signal; if the
  ranking is pure noise, PBO → 0.5 and the "best" configuration was selected
  by chance.

A final layer tests **regime dependence**: stationary bootstrap paths are
drawn from each structural regime (high-volatility shocks, rising rates,
prolonged sideways markets), and a survival rate reports the fraction of
paths where the strategy stays profitable. A strategy whose edge exists only
inside one regime is flagged rather than celebrated.

In the illustrative trials above, the configuration with the highest

ledger's out-of-sample record, not the in-sample frontier, is what survives.

## What the pipeline guarantees

A complete PySharpe run is a chain of explicit, auditable decisions:

1. Prices cached and FX-converted without lookahead bias.
2. Expected returns and covariances shrunk, not trusted.
3. A frontier optimized subject to Canadian tax reality — per account.
4. Every trial recorded, justified, and deflated against the multiplicity of
   experiments.

The portfolio that comes out the other end is not the one that looked best in
hindsight. It is the one that kept its rank when the ledger checked every
path it could have taken — tax drag minimized, overfitting refused.
