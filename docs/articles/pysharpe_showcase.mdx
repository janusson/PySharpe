# Three ETFs, Three Accounts: PySharpe's 2-D Asset Location Engine

*How a 45%-bracket Canadian investor should place VFV, QQC, and VDY across TFSA, RRSP, and Non-Registered accounts — and why "what to hold" is only half the optimization.*

---

Most portfolio optimizers answer one question: **what to hold**. Markowitz,
Black-Litterman, risk parity — all of them live in a single dimension of asset
weights. But a Canadian investor with a TFSA, an RRSP, and a Non-Registered
account is actually facing a **two-dimensional problem**: for every dollar of
VFV, there are three different places to put it, and the after-tax return is
different in every one of them.

PySharpe's Asset Location Engine solves both dimensions at once. This article
walks through the engine's mathematics on a concrete scenario — the same
scenario shipped in the repository as
[`scripts/test_location_matrix.py`](https://github.com/janusson/PySharpe/blob/main/scripts/test_location_matrix.py):

> **The investor.** 45% marginal tax rate. Account capacities: TFSA 25%,
> RRSP 35%, Non-Registered 40%. Three broad-market CAD ETFs:
>
> - **VFV** — S&P 500, CAD-wrapped, 1.2% dividend yield, 0.09% MER
> - **QQC** — NASDAQ-100, CAD-wrapped, 0.5% dividend yield, 0.20% MER
> - **VDY** — Canadian high-dividend equity, 3.5% yield (80% eligible), 0.22% MER

## The tax drag nobody sees: foreign withholding

Before optimizing, the engine prices every (asset, account) pair through three
drag channels: **foreign withholding tax (FWT)**, **income taxation**, and
**MER**. The FWT channel is where most Canadian investors — and most
optimizers — go wrong.

The US withholds 15% of dividends paid to foreign investors. Whether you get
that back depends on the fund's **domicile** and the account it sits in:

| Asset | TFSA | RRSP | NON_REG |
| ----- | ---- | ---- | ------- |
| **VFV** (CAD-wrapped US) | **−0.180%/yr** | **−0.180%/yr** | −0.000%/yr |
| **QQC** (CAD-wrapped US) | **−0.075%/yr** | **−0.075%/yr** | −0.000%/yr |
| **VDY** (Canadian) | −0.000%/yr | −0.000%/yr | −0.000%/yr |

*Annual FWT drag computed by `AssetLocationEngine.compute_fwt_drag()` at a 15%
treaty rate — the engine's actual output for this scenario.*

Two subtleties make this table worth reading carefully.

**First — the wrapper trap.** VFV and QQC are *Canadian-domiciled funds that
wrap US equities*. The fund itself pays the 15% withholding internally, and that
cost is **unrecoverable in every account** — including the RRSP. The famous
"US stocks belong in the RRSP" treaty protection only applies to **US-domiciled**
securities (like VOO) held directly. If you own the CAD wrapper, the RRSP buys
you deferral, not a withholding refund.

**Second — the foreign tax credit.** In the Non-Registered account the
withholding is recoverable through the foreign tax credit, which is why the
FWT column shows zero there. But nothing is free: foreign dividends in a taxable
account are then taxed as ordinary income at the full 45% marginal rate.

## What the engine actually computes

PySharpe's [`AssetLocationEngine`](https://github.com/janusson/PySharpe/blob/main/src/pysharpe/optimization/tax_location.py)
turns the tax code into arithmetic. At a 45% marginal rate:

```
eligible dividends:  MTR × 1.38 − (federal DTC + provincial DTC) × 1.38
                   = 0.45 × 1.38 − 0.250198 × 1.38
                   = 27.57%  ← effective rate, not the headline 45%

capital gains:      MTR × 50% inclusion = 22.50%
foreign income:     full MTR            = 45.00%
```

Now compare a dollar of dividends in each account:

- **VDY in NON_REG**: its 3.5% eligible yield is taxed at 27.57% →
  **0.96%/yr of drag**. In the TFSA or RRSP: **zero**.
- **VFV in NON_REG**: its 1.2% foreign yield is taxed at 45% →
  **0.54%/yr of drag** — three times its shelter drag of 0.18%/yr FWT.
- **QQC in NON_REG**: 0.5% yield × 45% → **0.225%/yr** — the cheapest
  taxable placement of the three.

The engine encodes this as tax-adjusted expected returns per (asset, account)
pair, then hands the whole **N × M matrix** — not just an N-vector — to the
Sharpe optimizer, together with MER drags (as decimal fractions, e.g. `0.0009`,
never percentage points) and hard per-account capacity constraints.

The matrix tells a three-sentence story:

1. **Shelter the yield.** VDY's 3.5% eligible dividend is the most
   tax-sensitive cash flow in the portfolio (0.96%/yr in NON_REG, nothing in a
   shelter), so it fills the TFSA first and takes as much RRSP as the 50%
   per-asset cap allows.
2. **Give US-wrapped equity the remaining shelter.** A VFV dollar in NON_REG
   costs 0.54%/yr (45% on a 1.2% foreign yield); in the RRSP it costs only the
   unrecoverable 0.18% FWT — a 0.36%/yr saving per sheltered dollar — so VFV
   takes the RRSP capacity VDY's cap leaves behind.
3. **Let the lowest-yield asset pay the taxable bill.** QQC's 0.5% yield makes
   it the cheapest resident of NON_REG (0.225%/yr, versus 0.075%/yr FWT in a
   shelter — the smallest shelter premium of the three), so it absorbs the
   entire 40% taxable capacity while its growth compounds at the preferential
   22.5% effective capital-gains rate.

## The Sharpe layer, and an honest caveat

Tax placement is the *location* half of the objective. The *allocation* half is
a max-Sharpe optimization over tax-adjusted returns, with Monte-Carlo warm
starts, per-asset weight caps, and account capacities as hard constraints:

Here is the honest part of the demonstration. Taxes are deterministic, but
expected returns are not. When the engine's synthetic two-year history makes
equities look unattractive, the Sharpe objective can dominate the tax logic
entirely — the reproducible script run concentrates the portfolio in VDY's
favorable sample path. That is not a bug: it is a reminder that **any**
optimization is only as good as the return estimates it is fed, and that
short, noisy samples produce overconfident estimates.

Which is exactly why PySharpe refuses to ship an allocation without its
validation ledger.

## Why this allocation is not overfit

Every strategy PySharpe produces is stress-tested against the two failure modes
that kill backtests: **serial correlation leakage** and **multiple-testing
bias**.

**Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation (CPCV).** Returns are serially
correlated, so standard k-fold cross-validation leaks information across
adjacent folds and inflates apparent skill. PySharpe's `PurgedKFold` removes a
purge buffer before every test fold, enforces an embargo between consecutive
test folds — strictly, for every fold including the last — and sizes both gaps
from the asset's **own autocorrelation decay**, not an arbitrary percentage.

**Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR).** A Sharpe ratio that looks good in isolation
may be exactly what the best of many noisy trials should produce: the expected
maximum Sharpe of N independent trials grows like √(2·ln N) (extreme-value
theory). The DSR deflates the observed Sharpe against that null, further
adjusting for non-normal returns (skew and kurtosis) and — critically — for
**autocorrelation**: Lo's variance-inflation factor θ deflates the Sharpe by
√θ before deflation, so a smoothed, serially correlated return stream cannot
masquerade as skill.

**Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO).** In-sample and out-of-sample
performance ranks are compared across the full trial ledger; a PBO near 0.5
means the in-sample ranking carries no information about out-of-sample
performance.

## The takeaway

Asset location is a second optimization dimension that generic tooling simply
does not model — and in Canada, where a CAD wrapper silently costs 15 basis
points a year of unrecoverable withholding even inside an RRSP, the dimension
is worth real money. PySharpe prices it exactly, optimizes it jointly with
allocation, and then subjects the result to the same overfitting defenses the
research literature demands.

The code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/janusson/PySharpe) — and the tax
engine, the validation ledger, and this exact scenario are all one command
away.
